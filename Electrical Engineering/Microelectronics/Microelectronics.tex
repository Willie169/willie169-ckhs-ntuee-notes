\ssc{Signals}
\sssc{Signal}
A signal is a function that conveys information about a phenomenon. Signal processing is usually most conveniently performed by electronic systems, and voltage and current signals are studied here.
\sssc{Transducer}
A transducer is a device that convert a signal to a voltage signal.
\sssc{Signal source}
A signal source is typically represented in the Thévenin form, a voltage source in a series connection with a resistance, or the Norton form, a current source in a parallel connection with a resistance.
\sssc{Symbol convention}
\bit
\item Lowercase character with uppercase subscript means total signal, e.g. $i_C(t)$.
\item Lowercase character with lowercase subscript means AC signal, e.g. $i_c(t)$.
\item Uppercase character with uppercase subscript means DC signal, e.g. $I_C$.
\item Uppercase character with lowercase subscript means peak signal, e.g. $I_c$.
\eit
Their relation is
\[i_C(t)=i_c(t)+I_C\]
\[i_c(t)=I_c\sin(\omega t+\varphi)\]
\ssc{Amplifier}
\sssc{Amplifier}
An electronic device which amplifies an electrical signal by a scale called amplifier gain $A$ and optionally convert voltage signal to current signal or vice versa.
\sssc{Circuit model of voltage amplifier}
A voltage amplifier draws current from a source $v_s$ with internal resistance $R_s$ with input resistance $R_i$ and voltage $v_{in}$ across it and supplies $v_c=\mu v_{in}$ to a load network with output resistance $R_o$ and load $R_L$.
\[\frac{v_{in}}{v_s}=\frac{R_i}{R_s+R_i}\]
\[\frac{v_c}{v_{in}}=\mu\]
\[\frac{v_{out}}{v_c}=\frac{R_L}{R_o+R_L}\]
Voltage gain:
\[A_v=\frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}}=\mu\frac{R_iR_L}{(R_s+R_i)(R_o+R_L)}\]
$\abs{A}<\abs{\mu}$ due to loading effects.

A good amplifier requires small loading effect:
\[R_i\gg R_s,\quad R_o\ll R_L,\quad A_v\approx\mu.\]
\sssc{Circuit model of current amplifier}
A current amplifier draws current from a current source $i_s$ with internal resistance $R_s$ in parallel with input resistance $R_i$ and current $i_{in}$ through it and supplies $i_c=\beta i_{in}$ by CCCS to a load network with output resistance $R_o$ in parallel and load $R_L$.
\[\frac{i_{in}}{i_s}=\frac{R_s}{R_s+R_i}\]
\[\frac{i_c}{i_{in}}=\beta\]
\[\frac{i_{out}}{i_c}=\frac{R_o}{R_o+R_L}\]
Current gain:
\[A_c=\frac{i_{out}}{i_{in}}=\beta\frac{R_sR_o}{(R_s+R_i)(R_o+R_L)}\]
$\abs{A}<\abs{\beta}$ due to loading effects.

A good amplifier requires small loading effect:
\[R_i\ll R_s,\quad R_o\gg R_L,\quad A_c\approx\beta.\]
\sssc{Circuit model of transconductance amplifier}
A transconductance amplifier draws current from a voltage source $v_s$ with internal resistance $R_s$ in series with input resistance $R_i$ and current $i_{in}$ through it and supplies $i_c=gv_{in}$ by VCCS to a load network with output resistance $R_o$ in parallel and load $R_L$.
\[\frac{v_{in}}{v_s}=\frac{R_i}{R_s+R_i}\]
\[\frac{i_c}{v_{in}}=g\]
\[\frac{i_{out}}{i_c}=\frac{R_o}{R_o+R_L}\]
Gain:
\[A_g=\frac{i_{out}}{v_{in}}=g\frac{R_iR_o}{(R_s+R_i)(R_o+R_L)}\]
$\abs{A}<\abs{g}$ due to loading effects.

A good amplifier requires small loading effect:
\[R_i\gg R_s,\quad R_o\gg R_L,\quad A_g\approx g.\]
\sssc{Circuit model of transresistance amplifier}
A transresistance amplifier draws current from a current source $i_s$ with internal resistance $R_s$ in parallel with input resistance $R_i$ and current $i_{in}$ through it and supplies $v_c=ri_{in}$ by CCVS to a load network with output resistance $R_o$ in series and load $R_L$.
\[\frac{i_{in}}{i_s}=\frac{R_s}{R_s+R_i}\]
\[\frac{v_c}{i_{in}}=r\]
\[\frac{v_{out}}{v_c}=\frac{R_L}{R_o+R_L}\]
Gain:
\[A_r=\frac{v_{out}}{i_{in}}=\beta\frac{R_sR_L}{(R_s+R_i)(R_o+R_L)}\]
$\abs{A}<\abs{r}$ due to loading effects.

A good amplifier requires small loading effect:
\[R_i\ll R_s,\quad R_o\ll R_L,\quad A_c\approx r.\]
\sssc{Amplifier gain in decibels (dB)}
A power gain $A_p$ in dB is defined as
\[G_p=10\log_{10}(A_p).\]
A voltage gain $A_v$ in dB is defined as
\[G_v=20\log_{10}(A_v).\]
A current gain $A_c$ in dB is defined as
\[G_c=20\log_{10}(A_c).\]
\sssc{Amplifiers in cascade}
Output of an amplifier as input of another amplifier.


\ssc{Operational Amplifier (op amp or op-amp)}
\sssc{Definition}
An op amp has five terminals:
\bit
\item $v_+$: non-inverting input voltage of the terminal at the $+$ sign;
\item $v_-$: inverting input voltage of the terminal at the $-$ sign;
\item $v_o$: output voltage of the terminal at the opposite side of inputs;
\item $v_{CC+}$: positive supply voltage, denoted on the $+$ side of an op-amp or omitted;
\item $v_{CC-}$: negative supply voltage, denoted on the $-$ side of an op-amp or omitted.
\eit
\[v_o=A(v_+-v_--v_{os}),\]
where $A$ is open-loop gain, and $v_{os}$ is input offset voltage.

\sssc{Ideal op amp}
\bit
\item Infinite open-loop gain, infinite bandwidth, and zero output impedance: $A=\infty$ and is independent of frequency and output voltage.
\item Zero input offset voltage: $v_{os}=0$.
\item No current flow into either of the input terminals.
\eit
This is a linear element with inputs $v_+$ and $v_-$ and output $v_o$.
\sssc{General amplifier with inverting configuration}
A general amplifier with inverting configuration consists of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_-$ through an input impedance $Z_i$, $v_+$ is connected to ground, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback impedance $Z_f$.
\[I_i=\frac{V_i}{Z_i}=I_f=\frac{-V_o}{Z_f},\]
\[V_o=-\frac{Z_f}{Z_i}V_i.\]
When $\abs{\Re(\frac{Z_f}{Z_i})}<1$, it's called an attenuator.
\sssc{Inverting amplifier}
An inverting amplifier consists of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_-$ through an input resistor $R_i$, $v_+$ is connected to ground, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback resistor $R_f$.
\[i_i=\frac{v_i}{R_i}=i_f=\frac{-v_o}{R_f},\]
\[v_o=-\frac{R_f}{R_i}v_i.\]
\sssc{General amplifier with noninverting configuration}
A general amplifier with noninverting configuration consist of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_+$, $v_-$ is connected ground through a ground impedance $Z_g$, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback impedance $Z_f$.
\[V_i=V_o\frac{Z_g}{Z_f+Z_g},\]
\[V_o=\qty(1+\frac{Z_f}{Z_g})V_i.\]
\sssc{Noninverting amplifier}
A noninverting amplifier consist of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_+$, $v_-$ is connected ground through a ground resistor $R_g$, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback resistor $R_f$.
\[v_i=v_o\frac{R_g}{R_f+R_g},\]
\[v_o=\qty(1+\frac{R_f}{R_g})v_i.\]
\sssc{Unity gain amplifier, unity gain buffer, or voltage follower}
A unity gain amplifier consist of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_+$ and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$.
\[v_o=v_i.\]
\sssc{Summing amplifier}
A summing amplifier consists of an op amp, input voltages $v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n$, $n$ input resistors $R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_n$, and a feedback resistor $R_f$, where for any $k\in\bbN\land k\leq n$, $v_k$ is connected to $v_-$ through $R_k$, $v_+$ is connected to ground, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through $R_f$.
\[i_k=\frac{v_k}{R_k},\]
\[\sum_{k=1}^ni_k=i_f=\frac{-v_o}{R_f},\]
\[v_o=-R_f\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{v_k}{R_k}.\]
When $R_k=R$ for all $k\in\bbN\land k\leq n$:
\[v_o=-\frac{R_f}{R}\sum_{k=1}^nv_k.\]
\sssc{Difference amplifier or differential amplifier}
A difference amplifier consists of a minuend $v_1$, a subtrahend $v_2$, and four resistors $R_1,R_2,R_3,R_4$ satisfying the condition
\[\frac{R_1}{R_2}=\frac{R_3}{R_4},\]
where $v_1,v_2$ are connected to $v_-,v_+$ through $R_1,R_2$ respectively, $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through $R_3$, and $v_+$ is connected to ground through $R_4$.
\[v_+=v_2\frac{R_4}{R_2+R_4},\]
\[\frac{v_1-v_-}{R_1}=\frac{v_--v_o}{R_3},\]
\[\frac{v_1}{R_2}-v_2\frac{R_4}{R_2+R_4}\frac{R_2+R_4}{R_2R_4}+\frac{v_o}{R_4}=0,\]
\[v_o=\frac{R_4}{R_2}\qty(v_2-v_1).\]
When $R_1=R_2=R_3=R_4$:
\[v_o=(v_2-v_1).\]
\sssc{Comparator}
A comparator is an op amp with $v_+$ and $v_-$ connected to the voltage sources to be compared, in which $v_o$ is high when $v_+>v_-$ and low when $v_+<v_-$.
\sssc{Inverting integrator}
An inverting integrator consists of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_-$ through an input resistor $R_i$, $v_+$ is connected to ground, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback capacitor $C_f$.

Time domain:
\[i_i=\frac{v_i}{R_i}=i_f=-C_f\dv{v_o}{t},\]
\[v_o=v_o(0^-)-\int_0^t\frac{v_i(\tau)}{R_iC_f}\dd{\tau}.\]
Laplace domain:
\[I_i=\frac{V_i}{R_i}=I_f=-C_fsV_o+C_fv_o(0^-),\]
\[V_o=\frac{v_o(0^-)}{s}-\frac{V_i}{R_iC_fs}.\]
\sssc{Inverting differentiator}
An inverting differentiator consists of an op amp where input voltage $v_i$ is connected to $v_-$ through an input capacitor $C_i$, $v_+$ is connected to ground, and $v_o$ is fed back to $v_-$ through a feedback resistor $R_f$.

Time domain:
\[i_i=C_i\dv{v_i}{t}=i_f=\frac{-v_o}{R_f},\]
\[v_o=R_fC_i\qty(v_i(0^-)-\int_0^tv_i(\tau)\dd{\tau}).\]
Laplace domain:
\[I_i=C_isV_i-C_iv_i(0^-)=I_f=\frac{-V_o}{R_f},\]
\[V_o=R_fC_i\qty(v_i(0^-)-sV_i).\]
\sssc{Active filter}
Given a (passive or active) filter $v_f$ of an input $v_s$ and an inverting amplifier, noninverting amplifier, or unit gain buffer enforcing $v_o=Kv_i$, by connecting the ground and $v_i$ of the amplifier to the two terminals of the $v_f$, the two terminals ground and $v_o$ become an active filter of $v_s$ enforcing $v_o=Kv_f$.
